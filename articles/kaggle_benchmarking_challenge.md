## What I Benchmarked

In 1955, sociologist and science-fiction author Dr. James Cooke Brown set out to test the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis — the idea that the structure of a language shapes the thoughts of its speakers. His answer was **Loglan** (Logical Language): an entirely engineered language with a strict mathematical property. Every grammatically valid Loglan sentence has **exactly one parse tree**. No dangling modifiers, no ambiguous prepositional attachments, no guesswork.

Natural human languages, by contrast, are ambiguity machines. Take the phrase *"Pretty little girls' school."* A competent English parser must consider at least **five distinct syntactic trees**, depending on whether *pretty* modifies *little*, the girls, or the school — and whether *little* scopes over *girls* or *girls' school*. In Loglan, phonetic grouping particles (`ge`, `ke…gu`) enforce explicit parenthesization in speech itself. There is always one reading.

That makes Loglan a uniquely powerful **stress test for language models**. Most popular LLM benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8k, HumanEval) suffer from data contamination — their test questions have leaked into pretraining corpora across the internet. Loglan offers something fundamentally different:

- Sentences can be **algorithmically synthesized** with arbitrary nesting depths, generating fresh test cases on demand.
- Ground truth is **mathematically verifiable** via single-path CFG parse trees — no subjective grading rubric.
- The language is rare enough in training data that models cannot rely on memorized patterns.

We constructed a **60-problem golden benchmark** across three categories using the 10,000-word Loglan Online Dictionary (LOD) corpus:

| Category | Cases | What It Tests |
|---|:---:|---|
| **Syntactic Disambiguation** | 20 | Can the model enumerate all English parse possibilities and verify the single Loglan parse? |
| **Predicate Slot Identification** | 20 | Can the model map entities to Loglan's strict argument positions ($x_1$ agent, $x_2$ patient, $x_3$ recipient…)? |
| **Translation Consistency** | 20 × 5 runs | Does the model produce identical translations across 5 repeated runs at $T = 0.3$? |

Every Loglan word generated by the models was cross-referenced against the **9,988 verified entries** in the LOD SQLite dictionary to compute an exact **Hallucination Rate** — the fraction of generated words that simply do not exist in the language.

## Models Evaluated

We tested six models spanning the full spectrum — from a 1.5-billion-parameter open-weights model running on a free Kaggle GPU, to frontier proprietary flagships:

| **Gemini 3.5 Flash** | Google | Live GenAI API | Lightweight Frontier |

Five of the six models ran with identical **two-tier RAG grounding**: a retrieval pipeline that feeds each prompt with relevant LOD lexicon definitions (18,766 entries) and canonical grammar articles scraped from `loglan.org` (769 textbook chunks from 28 sources including Brown's *Loglan 1*, case-tag theory papers, and particle disambiguation guides).

The sixth — **Qwen 2.5 1.5B Instruct** — ran in **zero-shot mode on bare hardware**, with no external knowledge retrieval at all. This was deliberate: it serves as a controlled baseline showing what happens when a model must rely purely on its pretrained weights.

## Discoveries

### 1. RAG Is Not Optional — It Is the Entire Difference

The single most striking result in our benchmark is the gap between grounded and ungrounded models.

Running Qwen 2.5 1.5B on a Kaggle GPU with no retrieval produces **33.3% overall accuracy**. The per-category breakdown is revealing:

- **Disambiguation: 5.0%** (1 of 20) — Without grammar rules explaining `ge` grouping and scope markers, the model cannot resolve formal ambiguity.
- **Predicate Slots: 0.0%** (0 of 20) — Complete failure. A model simply cannot guess the mathematical argument structure ($x_1$ through $x_5$) of constructed predicates it has never seen before.
- **Translation Consistency: 100.0%** (20 of 20) — The one bright spot: the model produces the same (incorrect) output deterministically.

Now compare that to the RAG-grounded frontier models: slot identification surges from **0% to 98.8–100%**, and overall accuracy jumps from **33.3% to 99.6–100%** (Gemma 4 31B, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Opus 4.6).

This is not a marginal improvement. It is the difference between a tool that is useless and one that is nearly perfect. And it suggests that for formal symbolic tasks, **retrieval grounding matters more than parameter count** — a 31B open-weights model with RAG outperforms a frontier model without it.

You can reproduce this exact experiment in Section 7 of our [Kaggle Notebook](https://www.kaggle.com/code/torrua/benchmarking-on-loglan).

### 2. The "Puppy vs. Book" Hallucination

This was our most unexpected finding, and it reveals something important about how language models actually process formal structures.

Consider this sentence from Chapter 4 of Brown's *Loglan 1: A Logical Language*:

> `La Djan, pa donsu leda sorme le cinkau`
> *(John gave his sister the puppy)*

The predicate `donsu` has three argument slots: $x_1$ (giver), $x_2$ (gift), $x_3$ (recipient). When we asked a model to extract arguments from the case-tagged variant (`Kao la Djan, pa donsu dio leda sorme beu le cinkau`), it correctly identified the Loglan tokens:

- $x_1$ = `la Djan` ✓
- $x_2$ = `le cinkau` ✓
- $x_3$ = `leda sorme` ✓

So far, so good. But when the model translated these tokens into English:

- `le cinkau` → **"the book"** (correct: *the puppy*, from `cinta` infant + `kangu` dog)
- `leda sorme` → **"the small boy"** (correct: *his sister*)

**What happened?** The model's pretraining data is saturated with English linguistics examples. In Penn Treebank annotations, in Chomsky and Fillmore syntax textbooks, in countless NLP tutorials, the canonical ditransitive example is *"John gave the boy a book."* That statistical prior was strong enough to override the explicit Loglan definitions sitting right there in the context window.

In natural language evaluation, this hallucination is invisible — *"John gave the boy a book"* is a perfectly fluent, perfectly plausible English sentence. You would never catch it without checking. But on Loglan's mathematically grounded lexicon, the error is immediately flagged: `cinkau` ≠ `bukcu` (book), `sorme` ≠ `cmalo mrenu` (small boy).

This is precisely why formal languages make such powerful AI benchmarks: they are **unforgiving microscopes for pretraining bias**.

### 3. The Modifier Stacking Blindspot

When analyzing ambiguous English phrases like *"Pretty little girls' school,"* all models routinely omitted one or two of the five valid parse trees. They especially struggled with tertiary readings — cases where *pretty* modifies *little* rather than the noun, or where *little* scopes over *girls' school* as a unit.

But here is the interesting contrast: when presented with Loglan equivalents (`le bilti cmalo nirli ckela` vs. `le bilti ge cmalo nirli ckela`), the explicit grouping particle `ge` guided every model to the exact intended parse hierarchy with zero confusion. The formal syntax did the heavy lifting that the models could not do on their own.

### 4. Hallucination Rate Correlates with Model Size — Until RAG Intervenes

Gemma 4 31B achieved a **0.000 hallucination rate** — it never generated a single fake Loglan word across its evaluated cases. Claude Opus 4.6, a frontier model roughly ten times larger, hallucinated at a rate of 0.106. The smallest model, Qwen 1.5B, hallucinated at 0.500 — every other word it generated was fabricated.

But hallucination rate is not purely a function of scale. Mimo v2.6 Flash, with RAG grounding, achieved 0.097 despite being far smaller than Claude. The retrieval pipeline acts as a factual guardrail that partially compensates for reduced parameter capacity.

## Why This Matters Beyond Constructed Languages

The patterns we found in Loglan have direct implications for production AI systems:

**1. AI-Generated DSLs and Intermediate Representations.**
Modern agentic workflows rely on generating JSON schemas, SQL queries, or custom domain-specific languages for tool use. Loglan demonstrates that eliminating syntactic ambiguity in these communication protocols can dramatically decrease hallucination rates — from 50% down to near zero with proper grounding.

**2. Deterministic Agent-to-Agent Communication.**
When multi-agent systems pass messages in natural language, ambiguity cascades through downstream agents. A zero-ambiguity formal substrate guarantees semantic preservation across arbitrary agent chains — exactly the property that Loglan was designed to provide.

**3. Structured Information Extraction.**
Predicate slot identification mirrors real-world IE tasks: extracting entities, roles, and conditions from legal contracts, medical records, or financial filings. The leap from 0% to 100% slot accuracy with RAG grounding suggests that retrieval-augmented architectures are not merely helpful for structured extraction — they are essential.
