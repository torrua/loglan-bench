## What I Benchmarked

In 1955, sociologist and science-fiction author Dr. James Cooke Brown set out to test the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis — the idea that the structure of a language shapes the thoughts of its speakers. His answer was **Loglan** (Logical Language): an entirely engineered language with a strict mathematical property. Every grammatically valid Loglan sentence has **exactly one parse tree**. No dangling modifiers, no ambiguous prepositional attachments, no guesswork.

Natural human languages, by contrast, are ambiguity machines. Take the phrase *"Pretty little girls' school."* A competent English parser must consider at least **five distinct syntactic trees**, depending on whether *pretty* modifies *little*, the girls, or the school — and whether *little* scopes over *girls* or *girls' school*. In Loglan, phonetic grouping particles (`ge`, `ke…gu`) enforce explicit parenthesization in speech itself. There is always one reading.

That makes Loglan a uniquely powerful **stress test for language models**. Most popular LLM benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8k, HumanEval) suffer from data contamination — their test questions have leaked into pretraining corpora across the internet. Loglan offers something fundamentally different:

- Sentences can be **algorithmically synthesized** with arbitrary nesting depths, generating fresh test cases on demand.
- Ground truth is **mathematically verifiable** via single-path CFG parse trees — no subjective grading rubric.
- The language is rare enough in training data that models cannot rely on memorized patterns.

We constructed a **60-problem golden benchmark** across three categories using the 9,988-word Loglan Online Dictionary (LOD) corpus:

| Category | Cases | What It Tests |
|---|:---:|---|
| **Syntactic Disambiguation** | 20 | Can the model enumerate all English parse possibilities and verify the single Loglan parse? |
| **Predicate Slot Identification** | 20 | Can the model map entities to Loglan's strict argument positions ($x_1$ agent, $x_2$ patient, $x_3$ recipient…)? |
| **Translation Consistency** | 20 | Does the model produce identical, deterministic translations under formal constraints? |

Every Loglan word generated by the models was cross-referenced against the **9,988 verified entries** in the LOD SQLite dictionary to compute an exact **Hallucination Rate** — the fraction of generated words that simply do not exist in the language.

The **Zero-Shot setups** run with zero external context, testing what raw pretrained weights achieve when isolated from external memory.

## Core Discoveries

### 1. The RAG Paradox: Grounding Helps Some Models and Hurts Others

The most unexpected finding in our benchmark: **RAG grounding does not universally improve performance**.

**Claude Opus 4.6** achieves **100.0% accuracy and 0.0% hallucination** in pure zero-shot mode — perfect scores across all 60 cases. But when we add two-tier RAG grounding, accuracy *drops* to 99.6% and hallucination rate *surges* to **10.6%**. The retrieval context floods the prompt with hundreds of LOD dictionary words, and Claude begins misapplying those words in its responses — a form of "context contamination."

**Gemini 3.8 Flash** shows the opposite pattern: RAG cuts its hallucination rate from **9.6% to 4.4%** — a 54% reduction. For Gemini, the external vocabulary acts as a stabilizing governor.

This asymmetry suggests that RAG's utility depends critically on a model's existing internal knowledge of the target domain. When a model already "knows" the domain well (as Claude Opus does for Loglan), external context can introduce noise. When it doesn't, RAG is indispensable.

### 2. Compact Models Suffer Total Slot Failure Without Grounding

On **Qwen 2.5 1.5B** running zero-shot, predicate slot identification accuracy drops to **flat 0.0%** (0 of 20 cases).

A model cannot guess whether the 3rd argument position of `donsu` represents the recipient or the gift without external documentation. But when open models are grounded with the LOD schema, slot accuracy jumps from **0.0% to 70.0%** (Mimo v2.6 Flash) and reaches **98.8%–100.0%** on frontier architectures. Retrieval grounding is not an incremental enhancement for structured extraction; it is the entire difference between zero capability and production accuracy.

### 3. The "Puppy vs. Book" Case: Why Models Substitute Frequency for Fact

During benchmark development, an analysis of Chapter 4 of Brown's *Loglan 1: A Logical Language* revealed a characteristic failure mode of autoregressive generation:
> `La Djan, pa donsu leda sorme le cinkau`
> *(Literal: John gave his sister the puppy / infant-dog)*
> - `sorme` = sister (Primitive)
> - `cinkau` = `cinta` (infant) + `kangu` (dog) = puppy / infant-dog (Complex)

When an LLM was asked to extract and translate argument slots for `donsu` ($x_1$: giver, $x_2$: gift, $x_3$: recipient), it correctly picked out the surface Loglan tokens:
- $x_1$ = `la Djan`
- $x_2$ = `le cinkau`
- $x_3$ = `leda sorme`

However, in the English translation, the model generated:
- `le cinkau` $\to$ **"the book"** (instead of *the puppy*)!
- `leda sorme` $\to$ **"the small boy"** (instead of *his sister*)!

**The fundamental difference between human reasoning and LLMs**:
Catching this discrepancy requires knowing or looking up word definitions (`cinkau` = puppy, `sorme` = sister). But once a human knows the definition, they would never substitute an unrelated concept simply because it is more common in speech. A human translator translates "puppy" as "puppy", regardless of how rarely people give puppies compared to books in natural conversation.

For an autoregressive model, however, a dictionary definition in context is merely a set of tokens competing for attention against massive pretraining priors. In English corpora, the ditransitive frame `John gave [X] a [Y]` completes with `the boy a book` millions of times. When that statistical prior is strong enough, the model defaults to the path of least resistance: it seamlessly substitutes the high-frequency trope for the factually defined word.

### 4. The Modifier Stacking Blindspot

When analyzing ambiguous English phrases like *"Pretty little girls' school"*, all models frequently omitted tertiary parses (e.g. failing to notice that *pretty* could modify *little*, or *little* could modify *girls' school*).

However, when parsing the Loglan expressions (`le bilti cmalo nirli ckela` vs `le bilti ge cmalo nirli ckela`), the explicit grouping particle `ge` allowed models to parse the exact intended hierarchy with zero confusion. Formal syntax compensates for what models struggle to resolve heuristically.

## Real-World Meaning: Why This Matters Beyond Constructed Languages

1. **AI DSLs and Intermediate Representations (IR)**:
   Modern agentic systems rely on generating JSON, SQL, or custom DSLs for tool use. Loglan demonstrates that eliminating syntactic ambiguity in communication protocols dramatically decreases hallucination rates.
2. **Deterministic Agent-to-Agent Communication**:
   When multi-agent systems communicate in natural language, ambiguity cascades through downstream agents. A zero-ambiguity formal substrate guarantees semantic preservation across arbitrary agent chains.
3. **Structured Information Extraction**:
   Predicate slot identification mirrors real-world information extraction (extracting entities, roles, and conditions from legal contracts or medical records). The 100% slot accuracy achieved with RAG grounding demonstrates the necessity of explicit schema grounding for reliable IE pipelines.
